Quick Recap

Every conditional statement P → Q travels with three relatives, built by two simple moves — swap the two halves, or negate them. Do both, in that order, and you get the contrapositive.

Everyday Version

P ∨ ¬P is "Either it's raining, or it's not raining" — always true, no matter the weather. That's a tautology.

The idea behind every law below

Logical Equivalence

Different Expressions  →  Same Meaning
Which Path Reaches the Library?

Two completely different routes — from the Classroom, or from the Hostel — both end up at the same place.

Classroom  →  Library      Hostel  →  Library
The two routes look nothing alike, yet they arrive at the exact same destination. That's precisely what logical equivalence means: two expressions can look completely different — P ∧ Q versus ¬(¬P ∨ ¬Q) — and still always "arrive" at the same truth value, row for row. Same with plain arithmetic: 5 ← 5+0 and 5 ← 3+2 — different-looking routes, same number at the end.
The law that unlocks all the others

The Conditional Law

P → Q  ≡  ¬P ∨ Q
Why This Matters

Every proof "without truth tables" you've stepped through leaned on this single rewrite — turning an arrow into a negation-and-or. Once → becomes ¬∨, all the algebra laws below become available to simplify it.

Law 1

Identity Laws

P ∧ T ≡ P   ·   P ∨ F ≡ P
Which Calculator Would You Choose?

Old → Slow!   vs   Modern → Extremely fast!

Obviously the modern one. Math has its own version of a "fast calculator" — a shortcut for two very common expressions:

P ∧ T   and   P ∨ F

Both shortcut straight to just P. Any statement!

The Arithmetic Behind It

Recall from ordinary arithmetic — the Identity Property:

  • ∧ behaves like multiplication (×), T behaves like 1
  • ∨ behaves like addition (+), F behaves like 0
P ∧ T := P × 1 = P      P ∨ F := P + 0 = P
Law 2

Domination Laws

P ∧ F ≡ F   ·   P ∨ T ≡ T
Attendance AND Principal's Approval

Students must satisfy Attendance AND the Principal's Approval.

p: Attendance requirement satisfied  ·  the Principal's Approval = False!

p ∧ F  ≡  F   (p × 0 = 0 = F)

It doesn't matter how good your attendance is — one guaranteed False dominates the whole conjunction.

Free Wi-Fi for Everyone!

Every student receives free Wi-Fi — = T, no conditions attached.

"No!" Why? You'll receive it anyway, right!
p ∨ T  ≡  T
One guaranteed True dominates the whole disjunction.
Two Ways to Feel It

P ∧ F ≡ F — switching off the main electricity to a building: it no longer matters whether the individual room switches are on. Every light stays off.

P ∨ T ≡ T — every student automatically passes the course: individual marks become irrelevant, the result is always true.

Telling them apart

Ask Yourself: "Does the Original Statement Survive?"

The Mnemonic

If Yes — the letters sit far apart (A…T: P ∧ T) → Identity. P survives.

If No — the letters sit closer together (P ∧ F / P ∨ T) → Domination. P gets overridden.

RuleFormula
IdentityP ∧ T ≡ P  ·  P ≡ P ∨ F
DominationP ∧ F ≡ F  ·  P ∨ T ≡ T
Law 3

Idempotent Law

P ∧ P ≡ P ∨ P ≡ P
Saying "Present" Twice
"No!" Does repeating the same fact create a new fact? "No!" Repeating the same statement — whether joined with AND or OR — adds absolutely no new information.
P ∧ P = P ∨ P ≡ P

Like pressing an elevator button that's already lit up — nothing changes.

Law 4

Double Negation

¬(¬P) ≡ P
"I'm Not Unhappy"

"I'm not unhappy"  →  "I'm happy," right?

¬(¬P) ≡ P

Two negations cancel each other out — just like flipping a light switch off, then on again.

Law 5

Commutative Law

P ∧ Q ≡ Q ∧ P   ·   P ∨ Q ≡ Q ∨ P
The Shoe–Socks Puzzle

Changing the order changes nothing.

Team A: Rahul & Priya   vs   Team A: Priya & Rahul
No! Only the order changed — the team itself is identical.

Just like ordinary arithmetic: 4 × 3 = 3 × 4 and 2 + 5 = 5 + 2.

Law 6

Associative Law

(P∧Q)∧R ≡ P∧(Q∧R)   ·   (P∨Q)∨R ≡ P∨(Q∨R)
Inter-Class Math Relay Race

Team: A, B, C — grouped two different ways:

1st: (A and B) and C      2nd: A and (B and C)

Did we change the team? Or merely the grouping?

Grouping, right! Changing the grouping changes nothing.
Don't Confuse These Two

Commutative → the order changes. Associative → the brackets change. Nothing else about either one.

Law 7

Distributive Law

P∧(Q∨R) ≡ (P∧Q)∨(P∧R)
"Today Everyone Gets Chocolates!"

Carry one box → to all.

P∧(Q∨R) ≡ (P∧Q)∨(P∧R)
P∨(Q∧R) ≡ (P∨Q)∧(P∨R)

One proposition (P, "the chocolate") gets shared across every branch — exactly like carrying one box and giving a piece to everyone in the room.

Law 8

Absorption Law

P∨(P∧Q) ≡ P ≡ P∧(P∨Q)
Every VIT Bhopal Student Has an ID Card

Statement: All VIT students  OR  all VIT students who wear spectacles.

Hint: the larger group "absorbs" the smaller one.

∀ P, Q:   P∨(P∧Q) ≡ P ≡ P∧(P∨Q)
The Ocean and the Bucket

Throw one bucket of water into a large ocean. Does the ocean become a different ocean? No — the bucket is completely absorbed.

P absorbs both P∧Q and P∨Q — the extra expression adds nothing new.

Law 9 · The finale

De Morgan's Law

¬(P∧Q) ≡ ¬P∨¬Q   ·   ¬(P∨Q) ≡ ¬P∧¬Q
Welcome to the Logic Escape Room!

p: fingerprint accepted  ·  q: face recognition accepted

Rule: the student must escape before the bell rings! The only exit door opens only if both electronic locks are unlocked.

The door opens only when p∧q is true.

What happens if the door doesn't open?

At least one lock failed!
∀ p, q:   ¬(p∧q) ≡ ¬p∨¬q
similarly:   ¬(p∨q) ≡ ¬p∧¬q
Negation's Job, Once It Steps Inside
  1. Break the brace (the outer negation splits open the brackets)
  2. Flip the connective (∧ becomes ∨, or ∨ becomes ∧)
  3. Negate each statement inside

That three-step job is the whole magic of negation.
