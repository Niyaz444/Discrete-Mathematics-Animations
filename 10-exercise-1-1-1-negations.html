Exercise 1.1.1

Write the negation of each statement below. Try it in the scratch box first, then reveal to compare.

Quick rules for negation.
  • To negate a sentence "X," the simplest form is "It is not the case that X" — usually shortened naturally, e.g. "X" → "not X."
  • Double negatives cancel: negating "will not be open" gives "will be open."
  • To negate an equation A = B, write A ≠ B.
  • Negating a sentence doesn't change whether it's a statement — even opinions can be grammatically negated, even though neither the original nor its negation has a fixed, objective truth value.
a

I visited her place on Sunday.

✓"I did not visit her place on Sunday."The simplest negation just inserts "not" before the main verb.
b

The market will not be open in the evening.

✓"The market will be open in the evening."The original sentence already contains a "not" — negating it a second time cancels it out (¬¬p ≡ p), rather than adding yet another "not."
c

The square of the integer n is divisible by the prime p.

✓"The square of the integer n is not divisible by the prime p."Negating a "divisible by" claim just adds "not" — no need to describe what it IS divisible by instead.
d

x² + 3 = 27

✓x² + 3 ≠ 27Negating an equation swaps "=" for "≠" — you're negating the whole claim, not solving for x. (Also worth noticing: as written, this sentence has a free variable x, so strictly it's an open sentence, not a statement, until x is fixed.)
e

The child is cute.

✓"The child is not cute."Grammatically straightforward — but worth flagging: "cute" is subjective, so neither this sentence nor its negation is actually a mathematical statement. You can still negate a sentence's grammar even when it fails to be a statement in the first place.
f

The apple is ripe.

✓"The apple is not ripe."Unlike (e), "ripe" usually has an agreed, checkable test (colour, firmness, taste) — so this one plausibly IS a statement, and its negation is a statement too.
