Name the three classifications a compound statement can fall into, based on its truth table.

2Lv3

Classify q ∨ ¬q without drawing a table.

TAUTOLOGYSame pattern as p ∨ ¬p — one of the two sides is always True.
3Lv3

Classify r ∧ ¬r without drawing a table.

CONTRADICTIONA statement and its negation can never both be True.
4Lv3

Classify p ↔ q. Is it a tautology, contradiction, or contingency?

CONTINGENCYTrue when p and q match (both T or both F), False when they differ — depends on the inputs.
5Lv4

Go back to your Entry Ticket Q2 (rain or no rain). Now, formally, what makes p ∨ ¬p a tautology rather than just "usually true"?

💡It's not just usually true — every single row of its truth table comes out True, with zero exceptions. That's the strict requirement for "tautology."
6Lv4

Is p → (p ∨ q) satisfiable? Is it a tautology? Explain both.

BOTH YESIt's a tautology (True in every row), and every tautology is automatically satisfiable too, since "True in every row" certainly includes "True in at least one."
7Lv5

Construct your own contradiction using p and q (not just p ∧ ¬p repeated). Explain why every row fails.

🧩 OPENSample: (p → q) ∧ p ∧ ¬q. If p is True and q is False, p→q itself fails — so the whole conjunction can never be True. Many valid answers.
8Lv2

True or False: every contingency is satisfiable.

✓ TRUEBy definition a contingency has at least one True row — that's exactly what "satisfiable" requires.
