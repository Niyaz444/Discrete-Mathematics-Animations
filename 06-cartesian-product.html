Cartesian Product

Where two sets stop being separate and start becoming coordinates. The final piece of the set theory foundation.

6.1

What Is a Cartesian Product?

Definition. The Cartesian product of A and B, written A × B, is the set of every ordered pair whose first entry comes from A and second entry comes from B: A × B = {(a,b) | a∈A and b∈B}.

Ordered is the key word. (1,2) and (2,1) are different elements of A×B unless the two happen to coincide — position matters here in a way it never did for plain sets.

|A × B| = |A| · |B|
A×B ≠ B×A

In general — swapping the sets swaps the coordinates of every pair.

A×∅ = ∅

You can't form a single pair if one side has nothing to contribute.

|A×B| = |A|·|B|

Multiplication, not addition — every element of A pairs with every element of B.

A×B×C

Extends naturally to ordered triples, and beyond, for three or more sets.

6.2

Three Real-Life Examples

Anywhere two independent choices combine into one paired result, that's a Cartesian product.

Chessboard Squares

Every square is named by a (column, row) pair — an element of {a,…,h} × {1,…,8}. "e4" is literally one ordered pair out of 64.

{a,…,h} × {1,…,8}

T-Shirt Orders

Sizes {S,M,L} and colors {red,blue,green} — every possible order is one pair from Sizes × Colors, 9 combinations total.

3 × 3 = 9 combinations

Enrollment Records

A university's enrollment table pairs (StudentID, CourseID) for every registration — each row is an element of Students × Courses.

Students × Courses

Two Grid Diagrams — Drag It Yourself

A Cartesian product isn't drawn with circles — it's a coordinate grid. Drag every pair into its true cell.

Diagram 1 — Place Every Pair in A × B
A = {1,2} (columns), B = {x,y} (rows). Drag each chip into the cell where its column and row match — or into the reject zone if it isn't a valid pair at all.
Placed correctly: 0 / 6
(1,x), (1,y), (2,x), (2,y) are the four real pairs — (x,1) has the order flipped, and (3,x) uses 3, which isn't even in A.
Diagram 2 — Place Every Pair in A × B
A = {a,b} (columns), B = {1,2,3} (rows). Same task, bigger grid.
Placed correctly: 0 / 6
(a,1), (a,2), (b,3) are real pairs — (1,a) is flipped, (c,1) uses c ∉ A, and (a,4) uses 4 ∉ B.
6.5

Two Counter-Examples

Two assumptions people carry over from plain sets that don't survive contact with ordered pairs.

False Claim

"A × B = B × A, just like A ∪ B = B ∪ A."

False — the Cartesian product is not commutative. With A={1,2}, B={x,y}: A×B = {(1,x),(1,y),(2,x),(2,y)}, but B×A = {(x,1),(x,2),(y,1),(y,2)}. Every single pair has its coordinates flipped — these are completely different sets of pairs (they only coincide when A=B). Union and intersection don't care about order; ordered pairs are built entirely around it.
False Claim

"|A×B| = |A| + |B|."

False — it's multiplication, not addition. With |A|=2, |B|=3: the correct count is |A×B| = 2×3 = 6, not 2+3 = 5. Every single element of A must be paired with every single element of B — that's the multiplication principle from counting, not a simple combined total.
