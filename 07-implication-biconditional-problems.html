Problem 9

Knights and Knaves

A says, "At least one of us is a knave," and B says nothing. Determine whether A and B are knights or knaves. (Knights always tell the truth; knaves always lie.)
Work it out first
Case 1 — suppose A is a knave. Then everything A says is false. A said "at least one of us is a knave" — if that's false, then neither of us is a knave, meaning A is a knight. But we assumed A is a knave. Contradiction!
So A cannot be a knave. A must be a knight.
Since A is a knight, A's statement is true: "at least one of us is a knave" really is true. A isn't the knave (A is a knight) — so it must be B.
Conclusion: A is a knight, B is a knave.
A = KnightB = Knave
Problem 10

Knights and Knaves

A says, "I am a knave or B is a knight," and B says nothing. Determine whether A and B are knights or knaves.
Work it out first
Case 1 — suppose A is a knave. Then A's statement is false. A said "I am a knave or B is a knight." For an OR to be false, both halves must be false — meaning "A is a knave" is false. But that directly contradicts our assumption that A is a knave!
So A cannot be a knave. A must be a knight.
Since A is a knight, A's statement must be true: "I am a knave or B is a knight." A is definitely not a knave (A is a knight), so for the OR to hold, the other half must carry it — B must be a knight.
Conclusion: A is a knight, B is a knight.
A = KnightB = Knight
