Topic 3 — Tautologies, Logic

18 QuestionsLevel 1 → 5Individual work
Working definitions. Classify any compound statement by scanning its full truth table:
  • Tautology — True in every row (e.g. p ∨ ¬p)
  • Contradiction (fallacy) — False in every row (e.g. p ∧ ¬p)
  • Contingency — True in some rows, False in others (e.g. p ∧ q)
Level 1

A. Recall & Identify

1

Define, in your own words, a tautology.

DEFA compound statement whose truth table shows True in every row — true no matter what truth values its parts take.
2

Define, in your own words, a contradiction (fallacy).

DEFA compound statement whose truth table shows False in every row — false no matter what truth values its parts take.
3

Define, in your own words, a contingency.

DEFA compound statement that is True in at least one row and False in at least one row — its truth value genuinely depends on the inputs.
4

Name the classic tautology built from a single variable p and negation.

✓p ∨ ¬p — the Law of Excluded Middle.
5

Name the classic contradiction built from a single variable p and negation.

✓p ∧ ¬p — the Law of Non-Contradiction.
Level 2

B. Understand & Explain

6

Explain, without drawing a table, why p ∨ ¬p must be a tautology.

💡p and ¬p always have opposite truth values, so exactly one of them is True in every row. "Or" only needs one True part — so it's satisfied every single time.
7

Explain why p ∧ q is a contingency, not a tautology or contradiction.

💡It's True when both are True (one row), but False in the other three rows. Since it's neither always-True nor always-False, it must be a contingency.
8

Explain why p → p is a tautology.

💡The conditional is only False when the antecedent is True and consequent False — impossible here since they're identical. Every row is True.
9

Why does classifying a statement always require checking every row of its truth table, not just one or two?

💡Tautology and contradiction are claims about every possible input. A single True row doesn't rule out a False row elsewhere — you need the full picture to be sure.

Level 4

D. Analyze

14

Without a full table, classify ((p → q) ∧ p) → q. (Hint: this is the pattern behind a very famous rule of inference.)

TAUTOLOGYWhenever p → q and p are both True, q must be True (that's what → guarantees) — so the whole implication never fails. This pattern is called Modus Ponens, and we'll meet it formally soon.
15

Classify (p ∨ q) ∧ ¬p ∧ ¬q without a full table. Explain your reasoning.

CONTRADICTION¬p ∧ ¬q forces both p and q False, which makes p ∨ q False — directly contradicting the first part of the conjunction. No row can satisfy all three pieces at once.
16

A statement is called satisfiable if it's True in at least one row. Is every contingency satisfiable? Is every tautology satisfiable?

✓ BOTH YESA contingency has at least one True row by definition, so yes. A tautology is True in every row, which certainly includes "at least one" — so yes, tautologies are always satisfiable too. Only contradictions are unsatisfiable.
Level 5

E. Create & Evaluate

17

Construct your own tautology using p, q, and at least two different connectives (not just p ∨ ¬p repeated). Prove it's a tautology with a truth table.

🧩 OPENSample: p → (p ∨ q). Whenever p is True, p ∨ q is automatically True too, so the conditional never fails; when p is False, the conditional is automatically True regardless. Every row is True. Many valid answers — check with a full 4-row table.
18

Critique: "If a statement is True when p and q are both True, it must be a tautology." Evaluate this claim.

✗ FALSEBeing True in one row says nothing about the other three. Example: p ∧ q is True when both are True, but False in the other three rows — a contingency, not a tautology. A tautology needs True in every row.
