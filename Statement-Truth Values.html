Question 1

Is the following sentence TRUE, FALSE, or CANNOT BE DECIDED?
"Every detective is smarter than Light Yagami."

Why: Light Yagami is a fictional character, and "smarter" has no single, shared, objective test that works across every real and fictional detective. There's no way to check this claim the way we check "7 is prime" — so it can't be assigned True or False. It fails to be a statement in the first place, which is why no truth value can be decided.
Question 2

Which of the following is NOT a mathematical statement?

Why: (b) is a command — it directs an action rather than asserting a fact, so it has no truth value. The other three are all declarative and checkable (all happen to be True).
Question 3

Can a mathematical statement be FALSE and still be called a statement?

Why: Yes. A statement only needs exactly one truth value — True or False. Being False doesn't disqualify a sentence from being a statement; "5 + 5 = 9" is false but still a perfectly good statement.
Class DiscussionGroups of Four

In groups of four, discuss the following question:
"Can a genius always think logically?"

Use examples from:

  • Mathematics
  • Daily life
  • Detective stories
  • Computer Science
  • Artificial Intelligence

Each group must defend its answer using reasoning, not opinions.

There's no single correct answer here — it's a discussion prompt, and your reasoning matters more than your conclusion. A strong answer usually touches:
  • Mathematics: even celebrated mathematicians have published "proofs" later found to contain logical gaps — raw brilliance doesn't guarantee error-free reasoning.
  • Daily life: cognitive biases (like overconfidence) affect everyone, including highly intelligent people — intelligence and bias-resistance are different things.
  • Detective stories: characters like L or Sherlock Holmes are written as flawless reasoners, but that's a fictional ideal — real cognition has blind spots fiction conveniently skips.
  • Computer Science / AI: a program can execute its logic flawlessly and still produce a wrong answer if its starting assumptions or data were flawed — valid steps from a bad premise still fail.
Most strong answers conclude: raw intelligence and consistent logical discipline are related but not identical — one doesn't guarantee the other.

Abstract ThinkingJustify Every Answer

Answer each question carefully. Do not guess.

Question 1

Can a false sentence still be a statement?

Why: Yes — truth and statement-hood are separate questions. A statement just needs exactly one fixed truth value; False qualifies just as well as True.
Question 2

Can every true sentence be a statement?

Why: No — "feeling true" isn't enough. A sentence also has to be declarative and objectively checkable. "What a lovely day!" might genuinely be true on a lovely day, but it's an exclamation, not a statement — its grammatical form disqualifies it regardless of how true it feels.
Question 3

Can nobody know whether a statement is true, yet it still be a statement?

Why: Yes — a statement only needs a truth value in principle. The Goldbach Conjecture ("every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes") has a definite truth value, but no one has proven which one it is, even after centuries of trying. "Unknown to us" is not the same as "no truth value."
Question 4

Can a statement become false in another universe? (Hint: think about changing the domain.)

It depends on what kind of statement it is:
  • Pure logical/mathematical truths, like 2 + 2 = 4, don't depend on physical reality at all — they'd hold in any universe with the same underlying logical rules.
  • Statements about the physical world, like "water boils at 100°C", depend on physical constants and laws — change the domain (a universe with different physics), and the statement could genuinely become False.
This is a preview of an idea we'll formalise soon: every statement (especially ones with variables) is implicitly evaluated over a domain of discourse — and changing that domain can change the truth value.
Question 5

Why does Mathematics prefer statements over opinions?

Mathematics builds long chains of reasoning where each step depends on the one before it. That only works if every claim has a truth value everyone can check the same way. Opinions have no shared, objective test — two people can disagree forever with no way to settle it. If proofs were built on opinions, a single disagreement could collapse the entire chain. Statements give mathematics a stable foundation to build on.
Pause – Review – ProceedSelf-Check Before Notation

Before moving to notation, verify that you can answer the following without looking back. If any answer is unclear, pause here — discuss with your partner. Only then proceed.

1. State the mathematical definition of a statement.

A statement (proposition) is a declarative sentence that is either True or False — never both, never neither.

2. What are the two necessary conditions?

(i) The sentence must be declarative — an assertion, not a question, command, or exclamation. (ii) It must have exactly one truth value, decided objectively.

3. Can a false sentence be a statement?

Yes — False is a valid truth value. A statement just needs one fixed truth value, not specifically True.

4. Why is x² = 9 not a statement?

It contains a free variable x with no fixed value. Until x is specified, we can't decide True or False — this makes it an open sentence, not a statement.

5. Give two examples and two non-examples of statements from daily life.

Examples (statements): "My school starts at 9 AM." · "It rained yesterday."
Non-examples (not statements): "Please pass the salt." (command) · "Is the bus late?" (question)
