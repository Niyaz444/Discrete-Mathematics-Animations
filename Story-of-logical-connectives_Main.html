3.3 · The simplest but most powerful connective

Negation

In electronics, this exact symbol is called an inverter — the triangle-and-bubble shape you'll find on real circuit diagrams.
Think Before Reading

Suppose

P: "Today is Monday."

Without learning any mathematics, how would you express "the opposite" of this statement? Write your answer below. Now think carefully. Is

Today is Tuesday

really the opposite? Or could it be Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday, or Sunday?

"Today is Tuesday" is a tempting first guess — but it's far too specific. If today isn't Monday, it doesn't have to be Tuesday; it could be any of the other six days. The true negation only needs to say one thing: "Today is not Monday." That single sentence stays true across Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday, and Sunday — because negation isn't about naming the opposite day, it's about flipping a truth value.
The Key Idea

Negation does not change the subject, the object, or the meaning of the sentence. It changes only one thing:

THE TRUTH VALUE

Think of a light switch.

ON ⇔ OFF

Negation performs exactly this logical switching.

Conjunction

This D-shaped symbol is the standard AND gate used on real circuit diagrams — flat back, rounded front.
The Hook — The Scholarship Challenge

"One student will receive this scholarship."

P: Attendance ≥ 90%   Q: Final Marks ≥ 90
P ∧ Q

"What does this symbol mean?" — "Both conditions must be satisfied?" — "Exactly."

The scholarship is awarded only when both conditions are fulfilled. This logical operation is called CONJUNCTION.

The Golden Rule

Think of conjunction as a security system. Imagine a bank locker requiring

    AND
  • Fingerprint

If even one requirement fails, the locker does not open. Mathematical conjunction works in exactly the same way. Both conditions must succeed.

Counter Example — "Almost True" is Still False

Students often argue,

"One statement is true. Shouldn't the conjunction be partially true?"

Mathematics says No. Consider

P: "You entered the examination hall." (True)
Q: "You submitted the answer script." (False)

Can the university award marks? No. Completing only one condition is insufficient. Conjunction demands both conditions simultaneously.

Disjunction

The curved shield shape is the standard OR gate symbol — its curved back is what tells engineers "at least one," not "exactly one."
The Hook — Free Refreshments!

Professor Na smiles and announces,

"Can we have both?" — "No! OR means only one."

OR

In everyday English, OR sometimes means choose exactly one. But in Mathematics, OR has a completely different meaning. Today, we shall discover one of the greatest surprises in logic.

The Golden Rule

Mathematical OR means

AT LEAST ONE

It never means "exactly one" unless explicitly stated. Always remember

At Least One

This single sentence prevents one of the most common mistakes in logic. So Professor Na's "No, only one" was actually the everyday English answer — mathematically, Tea, Coffee, or both would all satisfy "Tea ∨ Coffee."

A promise with only one way to break it

Implication

Circuit-wise, p→q is built from an OR gate with p inverted first (¬p ∨ q) — that little bubble on the input is the whole trick.
The Hook — Professor Na's Laptop Promise
If you score above 90 marks in the final examination,
then I shall give you this laptop.

"Challenge accepted!"

When exactly have I broken my promise?

Instead of guessing, let us investigate logically.

Think Before Reading

Suppose Professor Na made the following promise.

If you score above 90, then I will give you a laptop.

Now consider the following situations. For each: has the promise been broken?

  1. A student scores 95. Professor Na gives the laptop.
  2. A student scores 95. Professor Na refuses to give the laptop.
  3. A student scores only 40. Professor Na still gives the laptop.
  4. A student scores only 40. Professor Na gives nothing.
  1. Not broken. P (score>90) is True, Q (laptop given) is True — the promise holds.
  2. Broken! P is True, Q is False — this is the only way a conditional promise can fail.
  3. Not broken. P is False — the promise never said what happens below 90, so extra generosity doesn't violate anything.
  4. Not broken. P is False again — nothing was promised in this case either, so giving nothing is perfectly consistent.
The Secret Behind Implication

A conditional statement does not claim that P causes Q. Instead, it simply promises that whenever P is true, Q must also be true. Therefore, the implication fails only in one situation.

P = True    Q = False

That is exactly the situation where the promise has been broken.

Counter Example — Implication is NOT Causation

Consider the statement

If I wear a blue shirt, then 2 + 2 = 4.

Since 2 + 2 = 4 is always true, the implication is logically True. However, wearing a blue shirt does not cause mathematics to change. Implication is about logical truth, not physical cause and effect.

Most Common Mistake

Students often believe

P → Q

means

Q → P.

This is incorrect. Example: "If it rains, the roads become wet" does not imply "If the roads are wet, then it rained." Perhaps someone washed the road, or a water pipe burst. The reversed statement is called the converse, and it is generally not equivalent to the original.

Biconditional

This is an XNOR gate — an XOR gate with its output inverted. Engineers use it whenever two signals need to match exactly.
The Hook — The Secret Club

"Only official members may wear this badge."

"If I become a member, can I wear the badge?"

Professor Na replies,

YES

"What if someone is wearing the badge? Must they already be a member?"

Again, Professor Na replies,

YES

Notice carefully. Two promises are being made.

Member → Badge
Badge → Member

Both directions are true. This relationship is stronger than implication. It is called

BICONDITIONAL

Golden Rule

The biconditional is True whenever both statements possess the same truth value. That means

T, T → T
F, F → T

If one statement is True and the other is False, the biconditional becomes False.

Exclusive OR (XOR)

The extra curved line behind an OR gate is what makes it an XOR gate — the mark that rules out "both."
Light Switch

Suppose

p: The first switch is ON
q: The second switch is ON

Then

p ⊕ q

means

Exactly one switch is ON.

If both switches are ON or both are OFF, then

p ⊕ q

is false.

Scholarship Eligibility

Suppose a university announces:

A student will receive the scholarship if he or she is a sports champion or a cultural champion, but not both.

Let

p: The student is a sports champion
q: The student is a cultural champion

Then the condition is represented by

p ⊕ q.

Only one achievement qualifies the student — being both a sports champion and a cultural champion, strictly speaking, does not.

The Golden Rule

Mathematical XOR means

EXACTLY ONE

It never means

AT LEAST ONE

Exactly one proposition must be true.

The Proof — All Four Cases
"A student will receive the scholarship if he or she is a sports champion or a cultural champion, but not both."

This is an exclusive or (P ⊕ Q).
