Subsets

One set living entirely inside another. It sounds simple — until you have to prove it, or tell it apart from "belongs to."

Topic 4 of 6 · Set Theory Series
4.1

What Is a Subset?

Definition. A is a subset of B, written A ⊆ B, if every single element of A is also an element of B. Formally: A ⊆ B ⟺ ∀x (x∈A → x∈B).

Notice the definition is built entirely out of an implication — which is exactly why a false antecedent makes it vacuously true (more on that below).

A ⊆ B  ⟺  ∀x (x∈A → x∈B)
Subset
A ⊆ B

Every element of A is in B. B might have extra elements A doesn't.

Proper Subset
A ⊂ B

A ⊆ B, and additionally A ≠ B — B has at least one element A is missing.

Superset
B ⊇ A

Just A ⊆ B, written from B's point of view.

Reflexivity
A ⊆ A

Every set is a subset of itself — trivially true.

The most useful fact in this whole topic: to prove two sets are equal, show A ⊆ B and B ⊆ A. If every element of each is contained in the other, they must be exactly the same set. This "double containment" technique is the standard way mathematicians prove set equality.

4.2

Three Real-Life Examples

Anywhere a smaller category sits entirely inside a bigger one, you have a subset.

Dean's List Students

Every student on the Dean's List is enrolled at the university — but not every enrolled student makes the Dean's List. Dean's List ⊆ Enrolled Students.

Dean's List ⊆ Enrolled

Mammals Among Animals

Every mammal is an animal, but plenty of animals — reptiles, fish, insects — aren't mammals. Mammals ⊆ Animals.

Mammals ⊆ Animals

Apps on Your Phone

Every app installed on your phone is available on the app store, but the store carries millions of apps you'll never install. Installed Apps ⊆ Store Catalog.

Installed ⊆ Store Catalog
4.3

Three Mathematical Examples

For each pair, test whether the candidates land in B — then decide the overall verdict.

A = {x∈ℤ | x is a multiple of 4}   vs   B = {x∈ℤ | x is a multiple of 2}
Does A ⊆ B?
Yes. Every multiple of 4 is automatically a multiple of 2 (4k = 2(2k)) — no exceptions exist.
A = {x∈ℝ | −1≤x≤1}   vs   B = {x∈ℝ | −2≤x≤2}
Does A ⊆ B?
Yes. [−1,1] sits entirely within [−2,2] — a smaller interval nested inside a bigger one.
A = {perfect squares ≤ 100}   vs   B = {x∈ℕ | x≤100}
Does A ⊆ B?
Yes. Every perfect square up to 100 (1,4,9,…,100) is itself a natural number no greater than 100.

4.5

Two Counter-Examples

The two mistakes almost everyone makes the first time.

False Claim

"Since 1 ∈ {1,2,3}, it must also be true that {1} ∈ {1,2,3}."

False — this confuses ∈ with ⊆. The elements of {1,2,3} are exactly 1, 2, and 3 — not the set {1}. So {1} ∉ {1,2,3}. What is true is {1} ⊆ {1,2,3}, since the lone element of {1} (namely 1) is a member of {1,2,3}. "Element of" and "subset of" are different relationships, and mixing them up is the single most common subset mistake.
False Claim

"The empty set can't be a subset of anything — it has no elements to compare."

False — ∅ ⊆ A for every set A, and vacuously so. Recall A⊆B ⟺ ∀x(x∈A → x∈B). For A=∅, the statement "x∈∅" is never true for any x — so the implication "x∈∅ → x∈B" is true automatically in every case, exactly like "if 1+1=3, then unicorns exist" was true earlier in this course. A false antecedent can't be caught out, so ∅ ⊆ B holds for literally any B, with zero exceptions.
