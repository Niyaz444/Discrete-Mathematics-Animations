Topic 2 — Connectives

Joining Statements

10 quick-fire questions. We know what a statement is — today we start gluing them together with "not," "and," "or," and "if... then."

5 min
1Lv1

Recap: is "x − 1 = 6" a statement? Why or why not?

✗ NOIt's an open sentence — x is a free variable, so no fixed truth value exists yet.
2Lv1

In everyday speech, for "I'll pass and I'll celebrate" to be true, how many of the two parts need to actually happen?

BOTHBoth parts must hold. "And" is the strictest way to combine two claims — it demands everything.
3Lv1

True or False: the word "not" simply flips a statement's truth value.

✓ TRUETrue becomes False and False becomes True — nothing more complicated than that.
4Lv2

Guess: if p is True and q is False, do you think "p and q" is True or False?

FALSE"And" needs every part to be true. One False part is enough to sink the whole combined claim.
5Lv2

Same p True, q False — guess: is "p or q" True or False?

TRUE"Or" only needs one part to hold. Since p is True, the whole claim survives.
6Lv2

"Coffee or tea?" usually means pick exactly one. "Study hard or you'll fail" allows doing both (studying hard AND still somehow failing isn't the point — the "or" here isn't demanding exclusivity). Why might mathematicians dislike this inconsistency in English?

💡 IDEAA symbol needs one fixed meaning to build reliable truth tables. So logic picks one "or" as the default (allowing both) and gives the "exactly one" version its own separate symbol.
7Lv3

Predict: if a statement has 2 building-block parts (p and q), each True or False, how many total combinations of truth values are possible?

4Two choices for p × two choices for q = 4 combinations (TT, TF, FT, FF). This is why a 2-statement truth table always has 4 rows.
8Lv3

Why do you think mathematics needs the "allows both" version of "or" as the standard one, rather than "exactly one"?

💡 IDEAIt matches how "or" behaves in most careful reasoning and proofs — e.g. "n is divisible by 2 or 3" should stay true even for 6, which is divisible by both.
9Lv4

Consider: "If it rains, I carry an umbrella." If it does NOT rain, does this sentence force you to carry — or not carry — an umbrella?

NEITHERThe sentence makes no promise at all about what happens when it doesn't rain. It only commits to something in the "if it rains" case.
10Lv5

One-line guess: when we combine "not," "and," "or" in one long sentence, do you think order matters — the way it does with + and × in arithmetic?

🔮 PREVIEWYes — logic has its own order of operations, just like arithmetic. We'll pin down exactly which connective "binds tighter" today.
